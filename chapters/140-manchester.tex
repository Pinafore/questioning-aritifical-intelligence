While Chapter~\ref{ch:ir} introduced Ellen Voorhees's Cranfield
paradigm as a way of testing systems' ability to answer
information-seeking questions, this chapter introduces an
alternative---the Manchester paradigm. You have not heard of before
because I'm making it up right now.  So hopefully you'll indulge me a
little as I make a case for why it's a good name.

This chapter first explains why we picked the name---symmetry with
Cranfield and a nod to question answering mythology we mentioned in
Chapter~\ref{ch:civilization}---and argues why the Manchester paradigm is often more
effective than the Cranfield paradigm. This will matter for the
serious business of leaderboards (Chapter~\ref{ch:leaderboards}). But
until then, this chapter spends time on fun, silly trivia games played
by college nerds and the complicated rules they have made up over the
years, which help us determine how smart an \abr{ai} is.

I'm spending this time on these silly trivia games because I think
they tell us something important about how to evaluate \abr{ai}.
%
We can't always sit down and have a conversation with an \abr{ai}
\textit{a la} the Turing Test (Chapter~\ref{ch:turing}), so we need to
have an automatic evaluation that feels more like the Cranfield
paradigm's reusable test collections (Chapter~\ref{ch:ir}).
%
The problem with Cranfield test sets---as we will see in
Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets} in detail with Google's Natural
Questions---is that naturalistic questions from real users can't probe like the \emph{Turing Test}.
%
Thus, the Manchester paradigm tries to take the best of both worlds:
reusable settings for \abr{ai} evaluation to probe how capable
\abr{ai} is efficiently without a human in the loop.
%
Can you get something close to a Turing Test on the cheap?  
%
I think you can, and that's why we should pay attention to the qualities of the questions in the Manchester paradigm.

\section{Two British University Towns Alike in Question Answering}
\label{sec:manchester:name} 

If the Cranfield paradigm got
\abr{ai} to crawl, the Manchester paradigm got it to
run.\footnote{Query whether we want \abr{ai} to be moving too quickly;
  see Chapter~\ref{ch:sci_fi}.}
%
Because this chapter is introducing the Manchester paradigm---which
prioritizes probing intelligence through carefully tailored questions---we
will keep the Cranfield discussion light here and focus on why the
Manchester framing matters for evaluation.

\subsection{Why the Name Manchester}
\label{sec:manchester:why-name}

We choose ``Manchester'' for symmetry with ``Cranfield'', they're both
British university towns.  But there are many British towns and cities
with universities.  Pedro Rodriguez and I picked Manchester because
Manchester also connects to three strands of question answering
history: teaching, comparing, and probing.
%
I'll go through these briefly here to connect with previous chapters
and to connect with what we will discuss later in the chapter.

\paragraph{To Teach: The Sphinx and the Socratic Method}
Manchester's Regiment used the Sphinx as its symbol~\cite{farmer-01}, and the
Sphinx riddle tradition underscores questions that test the answerer more than
they inform the asker (Chapter~\ref{ch:civilization}).
In Greek myth, the Sphinx asks everyone who enters the city a riddle that is
explicitly designed to test the answerer~\cite{renger-13}. The point of the
riddle is not information seeking: the Sphinx does not need to know what creature finishes its life on three legs.
%
Rather, the Sphinx's riddle is meant to highlight the three phases of \OE{}dipus's life: in youth he cursed, in adulthood he suffered the consequences, and in his blind dottage he dispensed advice that nobody listened to at Colonus.
% JBG(todo): I did this from memory, check and add citations
We also covered in a previous chapter how targeted questions awaken
understanding via the Socratic method, where questions guide the
answerer to insight~\cite{trepanier-17}.

\paragraph{To Compare: Granada Studios and \textit{University Challenge}}
Manchester's Granada Studios created a quiz show called
\textit{University Challenge}, whose format and history make it a useful
template for competitive question answering~\citep{taylor_mcnulty_meek}. For
the broader lineage connecting \textit{University Challenge}, \textit{College
Bowl}, and \quizbowl{}, see \citet{baber-15}.
%
The host knows the answer, so the goal is not to satisfy a
user's information need. Instead, the goal is to see which four students---the
ones from Balliol College or Sheffield---are smarter.
%
In the United States, this unique competition is called \quizbowl{}, and it has unique properties that inform the development and evaluation of \abr{ai}.
%
This chapter talks about how the philosophy of \quizbowl{} can inform
the authoring of individual questions.
%
Later, in Chapter~\ref{ch:leaderboards}, I discuss how the philosophy
of \quizbowl{} tournaments can improve \abr{ai} evaluation.

\paragraph{To Probe: The Turing Test}

Alan Turing was in Manchester when he proposed his imitation game~\citep{manchester-turing-25}, which reframes intelligence as a question-driven
evaluation  (Chapter~\ref{ch:turing}).
% 
But the judge asking the questions epitomizes the Manchester paradigm: devising questions that---given the skills shown and topics discussed---are most likely to trip up a computer.
%
This need to expose an answerer's weaknesses also shapes the craft of
question writing (Section~\ref{sec:manchester:craft}).

To recap, the Manchester paradigm emphasizes questions designed to
probe for specific capabilities. This does not replace user-centered
evaluations \textit{a la} Cranfield; instead it makes explicit that
some datasets and leaderboards are better understood as tests of
competence rather than proxies for user satisfaction.

Having already covered ancient Greece's connection to the Manchester
paradigm, we will first turn to Manchester's University Challenge
before returning to the legacy of the probing questions from the
Turing Test.

\subsection{\quizbowl{} Lineage and the \textit{University Challenge} Link}
\label{sec:manchester:qb-lineage}

My North American readers will at this point be shouting that I have
failed to mention Don Reid.
%
After returning from World War II, Reid drew on \abr{uso}
morale-building activities to develop the format with its first host,
Allen Ludden.  \textit{College Bowl} premiered on radio in 1953 and
moved to television in 1959~\citep{baber-15}.
%
Even keeping score took practice: Grant Tinker, later president of
\abr{nbc}, was the game's first scorekeeper, and Ludden sometimes had
to ad lib while Tinker caught up with the unfamiliar scoring
system~\citep{baber-15}.

\textit{College Bowl} shares a family resemblance\footnote{And like all families, there's
  some drama here about who came up with the idea first.  I think that
  Don Reid deserves the credit, but I'm foregrounding Manchester to
  emphasize the links with Turing.} with \textit{University
  Challenge}: both are competitive, knowledge-centered formats in
which the asker already knows the answer, and the goal is to compare
answerers rather than to serve a user. More importantly, their
questions are designed to discriminate who knows the most by having
players ``buzz in'' to signal that they know the answer.

From these beginnings as television shows on opposite sides of the
Atlantic spawned a massive community of people who write questions,
play on those questions, and watch others play on those questions.
%
As I mention in the introduction (Chapter~\ref{ch:introduction}), I
count myself as part of this community: it's where I met my wife, and
it provides much of my social network today.

One of the reasons I wanted to write this book is so that I could share this history with \abr{ai} community.
%
The format for comparing answerers was
developed by people trying to make a good game, long before it became
an evaluation task for \abr{ai}.
%
And despite my best efforts, these lessons have not been fully adopted by the \abr{ai} community.
%
Despite the complicated structure of these questions, I think there
are valuable insights from the details: when players can interrupt,
how clues are ordered, and how writers make the competition fair.  I
turn to those details next.

\subsection{Discriminative Questions and Incremental Evidence}
\label{sec:manchester:qb-discriminative}

\paragraph{Interruptible}

\quizbowl{}'s defining property is interruptibility: questions are read aloud and
can be answered at any time. Each word is a chance to demonstrate knowledge.
This makes \quizbowl{} maximally discriminative, rewarding knowledge rather than
buzzer speed or luck.
%
Once someone knows the answer, they use a
signaling device to ``\emph{buzz in}.'' If the player who buzzed is right, they
get points; otherwise, they lose points and the question continues for the
other team.

Not all trivia games with buzzers have this property: we'll talk about
the criticism Ken Jennings offered of the \jeopardy{} buzzer
system~\cite[he first earned his buzzer callouses playing \quizbowl{}
at Brigham Young University in Provo, Utah]{jennings-06}.
%
But unless the questions are exquisitely designed, you'll end up with
a test of reflexes, knowledgeable players will simply buzz when they
believe they have a greater than fifty percent chance of answering the
question correctly.
%
This is not just a bad test of intelligence (since it relies on
reflexes) but also bad television.
%
Thus, the questions used in \textit{University Challenge} are \textbf{pyramidal}.

\paragraph{Pyramidal}
\label{sec:manchester:pyramidality}

The goal of a question is to discriminate who knows more about a
topic: the smarter team should on average get the question right.
%
\textit{University Challenge} questions do a better job than ``stand alone'' questions
because discrimination happens \emph{within} a question: after every
word, an answerer must decide if they know enough to answer. \qb{}
questions are arranged to be maximally \emph{pyramidal}: questions
begin with hard clues that require deep understanding and proceed to
more accessible clues.

\begin{figure}
\question{With Leo Szilard, he invented a doubly-eponymous refrigerator with no moving parts. He did not take interaction with neighbors into account when formulating his theory of heat capacity, so Debye adjusted the theory for low temperatures. His summation convention automatically sums repeated indices in tensor products. His name is attached to the A and B coefficients for spontaneous and stimulated emission, the subject of one of his multiple groundbreaking 1905 papers. He further developed the model of statistics sent to him by Bose to describe particles with integer spin. For 10 points, who is this German physicist best known for formulating the special and general theories of relativity?}

\caption{An example \quizbowl{} question: the clues go from difficult
  to easy.  When the player (listener) knows the answer, they can buzz
  in to signal that they want to answer.  This procedure helps better
  evaluate who knows more about a subject, as every word helps
  discriminate who knows more about a topic.}

\label{fig:ex-qb}
\end{figure}

You might be scared off by how long the questions like the one in
Figure~\ref{fig:ex-qb} are, but in real \qb{} tournaments they are not
finished because the questions are \emph{interruptible}.
%
While few people know about the refrigerator Einstein designed (even
physicists), most practicing physicists do know about his summation
convention, and those familiar with the history of science probably
know about his 1905 \textit{annus mirabilis} that lead to his Nobel
prize.  Finally, just about everyone knows about his connection to the
theory of relativity.

Bringing this back to how we want to automate the Turing Test, pyramidality and interruptability make the process of answering a question more like a conversation.
%
You need to listen to what's being said, and when you've heard enough you give your answer.
%
Your timing and your judgement matters, and that reveals as much---and sometimes more---than your precise answer.

\subsection{Adjudication and Protests as Quality Control}
\label{sec:manchester:qb-adjudication}

Format is necessary but not sufficient. \quizbowl{} works because the community
invests in question quality and adjudication. In the early 1990s, the Academic
Competition Federation (\abr{acf}) formalized norms for clarity, specificity,
and fair evaluation. Their rules are designed to avoid ambiguity and to make
answer expectations explicit (e.g., when a day and month are required). This
aligns with modern critiques of ambiguity in \abr{qa} datasets.

% todo(jbg): Add ACF cite

The community also developed protest and adjudication norms that recognize
legitimate answer variants. That is directly relevant to machine \abr{qa}:
accepting only one surface form (e.g., ``Timothy Donald Cook'' but not ``Tim
Cook'') is both unfair and counterproductive. As we discuss in
Section~\ref{sec:manchester:craft}, answer correctness is often about
equivalence, not exact matching.

For example, whe I appeared on \jeopardy{}, I was initially ruled
incorrect for answering \underline{endoscope} to \question{Your
  surgeon could choose to take a look inside you with this type of
  fiber-optic instrument.}  I didn't have to do anything: a ``player
advocate'' (a staple of California gameshows after the cheating
scandals of the 1950s that we discuss in
Chapter~\ref{ch:leaderboards}) jumped into action, and the issue was raised to a group of on-call judges off site.\footnote{I haven't been able to find a source to support this, but I think this is to not just keep the filming studio less crowded but also to prevent subtle biases from affecting the judges: they only hear the candidate answer.}
\jbgcomment{Ping Clair McNair for her take on this %jbg(todo)}
}
After, judges ruled the response acceptable because \underline{endoscope} is a
more general term that includes \underline{laparoscope}.\footnote{\url{http://www.j-archive.com/showgame.php?game_id=6112}}
This is not a pedantic exception; it is a model of quality control that
acknowledges legitimate equivalence.


In high school and college national competitions and game shows,
if low-level staff cannot resolve the issue by throwing out a single
question or accepting minor variations (\underline{America} instead of
\underline{\abr{usa}}), the low-level staff contacts the tournament
director.
The tournament director, who has a deeper knowledge of rules and questions, often decide the issue.
If not, the protest goes
through an adjudication process designed to minimize
bias:\footnote{\smallurl{https://www.naqt.com/rules/\#protest}}
write the summary of the dispute,
get all parties to agree to the summary,
and then hand the decision off to mutually agreed experts from the tournament's phone tree.
The substance of the disagreement is communicated (without identities), and the experts apply the rules and decide.


The need for a similar process seems to have been well-recognized
in the earliest days of \qa{} system bake-offs such as \abr{trec-qa}, and \citet{voorhees-08} notes that
\begin{quote}
    [d]ifferent \abr{qa} runs very seldom return exactly the same [answer], and it is quite difficult to determine automatically whether the difference [\dots] is significant.
\end{quote} 
In stark contrast to this, \qa{} datasets typically provide only a single
string or, at best, a short list of strings. A correct answer then means
\emph{exactly} matching these strings or having a high token overlap
\fone{}. That framing treats answer equivalence as string similarity, but
humans routinely accept paraphrases, aliases, and answers with the right level
of specificity. The experiments we and others have run make the same point at a
high level: exact matching underestimates correctness, while purely semantic
matching can overestimate it. In other words, answer equivalence is not a
single number; it is a set of rules and adjudications that depend on context.

\paragraph{Well-Edited}

\qb{} questions are created in phases. First, the \emph{author} selects the
answer and assembles (pyramidal) clues. A \emph{subject editor} then removes
ambiguity, adjusts acceptable answers, and tweaks clues to optimize
discrimination. Finally, a \emph{packetizer} ensures the overall set is
diverse, has uniform difficulty, and is without repeats.

\paragraph{Unnatural}
\label{sec:manchester:unnatural}

Trivia questions are fake: the asker already knows the answer.
 But they are no
more fake than a course's final exam, which---like leaderboards---is designed
to test knowledge.
%
Because experts know when questions are ambiguous, they won't ask questions like \question{what play has a character
whose father is dead}.
The answer could be \textit{Hamlet}, \textit{Antigone}, or
\textit{Proof} (among others I am not thinking of), a good writer's knowledge avoids the ambiguity. When authors
omit these cues, the question is derided as a ``hose''~\citep{2013-eltinge},
which robs the tournament of fun (Section~\ref{sec:fun}).

This is not just an academic concern: Natural Questions, which we discuss in Section~\ref{sec:nq:problems}, had this property: it had ambiguous questions and arbitrarily selected one of the possible answers as correct.
%
This arbitrary reward not only is scientifically bad (you don't know which \abr{ai} is the smartest) it also is potentially injecting cultural biases into our \abr{ai}s (when in doubt, name the American play).


\paragraph{Complicated}

\qb{} is more complex than other datasets. Unlike other datasets where you just
need to decide \emph{what} to answer, in \qb{} you also need to choose
\emph{when} to answer the question.\footnote{This complex methodology can be an
  advantage. The underlying mechanisms of systems that can play \qb{} (e.g.,
  reinforcement learning) share properties with other tasks, such as
  simultaneous translation for both feature-based systems~\citep{grissom:he:boyd-graber:morgan-2014}
  and neural models~\citep{ma-etal-2019-stacl},
  human incremental processing in psycholinguistic theory~\citep{levy-08}
  and empirical reading behavior~\citep{levy-11}, and opponent
  modeling~\citep{he-16}.}
While this improves the dataset's discrimination, it can hurt popularity
because you cannot copy/paste code from other \abr{qa} tasks. The cumbersome
pyramidal structure complicates\footnote{But does not necessarily preclude, as
  the Illinois High School Scholastic Bowl Coaches Association shows:
\begin{quote}
    This is the smallest counting number which is the radius of a sphere whose volume is an integer multiple of $\pi$. It is also the number of distinct real solutions to the equation $x^7-19x^5=0$. This number also gives the ratio between the volumes of a cylinder and a cone with the same heights and radii. Give this number equal to the log base four of sixty-four.
\end{quote}} some questions (e.g., what is log base four of sixty-four).


\section{Specifically Testing Computer Ability}

While the Manchester Paradigm is inspired by human-derived tests,
in the \abr{ai} community, questions that specifically test computer ability are called adversarial examples.
An early example\footnote{Though they don't use the term ``adversarial''} is \citet{biggio-13}, who showed how to modify inputs
at test time to fool classifiers, including an illustration using
\abr{mnist} handwritten digits (Figure~\ref{fig:manchester:biggio-mnist}).\footnote{\abr{mnist} was built from
  \abr{nist}'s handwritten-digit collections, another connection to the
  reusable test collections of Chapter~\ref{ch:ir}~\citep{lecun-98}.}
\citet{szegedy-14} showed that even changes imperceptible to a human
could cause neural networks to misclassify images
(Figure~\ref{fig:manchester:szegedy-mnist}).

However, my favorite adversarial example is a real-life 3D-printed
turtle from Kevin Kwok and his collaborators~\citep{athalye-18}
(Figure~\ref{fig:manchester:adversarial-turtle}).\footnote{Because this is
  a chapter about the joy of human question answering including \qb{},
  I would be remiss if I didn't mention Kevin's connection to \qb{}.
  Back in 2011, Brianna Satinoff and I created a place people could play
  questions online. We used it to collect data on how well humans could
  answer questions. After we got the data we needed, we took the site
  down. Kevin and Ben Vest created a (much snazzier) version called
  Protobowl, announced in 2012~\citep{kwok-12-protobowl}. We collaborated
  on using its data to see how an \abr{ai} \qb{} player could adjust its
  buzzing strategy to its opponent~\citep{he-16}.}

I got into the business of writing adversarial questions when our \abr{ai} system beat Ken Jennings in 2015 in a quiz bowl match.
%
Ken Jennings is now the host of the trivia show \jeopardy{} and one of the most successful \abr{qa} players of all time: he was the first to win 74 games in a row on the show, he decisively won a ``\abr{goat}'' (The Greatest of All Time) tournament, and also was a dominant quiz bowl player in his day.
%
This pedigree is also why he was chosen to face off against Watson, as we detail in the next chapter.
%
I knew how badly our system sucked, and I knew how smart Ken is.
%
It was not smarter than Ken Jennings, but it was able to beat him by rote memorization and mechanical speed.

The questions were to blame.  
%
Even though the questions were written in the \qb{} style, they did not actually follow the pyramidal structure.
%
If computers \emph{and} humans were answering the questions, the clues should be ordered in decreasing difficulty for both players.
%
This was clearly not the case: we had obscure but easily looked up places in the first lines, quotes from poems, and math problems copy pasted from Wikipedia examples.
%
They were truly difficult for humans, but trivial for computers.

Taking a cue from the vision community, we wanted to think about how we could write questions that were actually pyramidal for both.
%
One problem is that creating a visual adversarial example is easy: you can just tweak a few pixels: increase the red here, decrease the blue there, etc.
%
These are easy to create because they are just tweaking the input that are imperceptible to a human.
%
However, you can't make these slight changes to a word: while pixels are continuous, words are discrete.

So we made the writing process interactive. In TrickMe, a \qb{} author
picked an answer and drafted a tossup while the system continually answered
alongside them~\citep{wallace-19} (Figure~\ref{fig:manchester:trickme-interface}).
The author wrote the question in one part of the interface, watched the model's
top guesses in another, and saw highlighted ``evidence'' for why the model
preferred those answers. If the model found the right answer too early, the
author could rewrite the clue, replace a stock phrase, or push the giveaway
later. The target was not gibberish that merely broke a classifier; it was a
normal, playable \qb{} question that remained fair to humans---i.e., respecting the norms of the Manchester Paradigm---while delaying or
defeating the machine.
%
Dynabench from Facebook (now Meta) later generalized this model-in-the-loop
pattern into a platform for dynamic data collection across tasks well beyond \qb{}
(Figure~\ref{fig:manchester:dynabench-interface}) and made it much more professional.

To me, this is how you scale up the Turing Test.
%
You can't have one interrogator for every model that comes out, but if you can craft questions that fool most of the models most of the time, you create something that can reveal if future models have made progress.
%
And because \qb{} questions are efficient: each clue helps determine ability, you need fewer of these questions to get a good estimate of models' ability.

One of the trivia experts
consulted in \citet{wallace-19} crafted a question that tripped up neural
\abr{qa} by embedding the phrase ``this author opens Crime and Punishment'' into
a question; the top system confidently answers \underline{Fyodor Dostoyevski}.\footnote{I wanted to say a little about the process of publishing this work and Eric, the brilliant student who worked on this. by this author opens \textit{Crime and Punishment} to find it has become
\textit{The Brothers Karamazov}}.
%

linguistic dexterity of the trivia community.

Of course, TrickMe was not the only way to turn adversarial evaluation into a
data-collection interface. \abr{fever}2 built adversarial fact-verification
examples through a shared task~\citep{thorne-19}, while Fool Me Twice used a
game-like setting for adversarial entailment~\citep{Eisenschlos-21}. Beat the
\abr{ai} put models in the loop for reading comprehension~\citep{bartolo2020beat},
and Adversarial \abr{nli} did the same for natural language
inference~\citep{nie2019adversarial}. Adversarial \abr{vqa} extended the idea
to vision-and-language questions~\citep{adversarialvqa}; later systems explored
synthetic amplification of human-written adversaries~\citep{bartolo2021improving}
and human--\abr{ai} hybrid attack construction~\citep{you-lowd-2022-towards}.
Dynabench packaged this model-in-the-loop pattern as reusable infrastructure
across tasks~\citep{kiela2021dynabench}. \abr{advqa} later asks whether the
resulting questions remain truly adversarial as models improve over
time~\citep{sung-etal-2025-benchmark}.
One of the benefits of contrived formats is a focus on specific phenomena that are difficult for players, whether they are human or computer.
\citet{dua-19} exclude questions an existing \abr{mrqa} system could answer to
focus on challenging quantitative reasoning.

A counterargument is that Cranfield paradigm questions---e.g., on Yahoo!
Questions~\citep{szpektor-13}, Quora~\citep{iyer-17} or web
search~\citep{kwiatkowski-19}---ignore the craft of question writing. Real
humans react to unclear questions with confusion or divergent answers,
explicitly answering with how they interpreted the original question (``I
assume you meant\dots'').
The later discussion of Natural Questions shows why this matters for real user
queries (Section~\ref{sec:nq:problems}). Given real-world applications must
cope with this noise, our systems must cope with it too. However, addressing
the real world cannot happen by glossing over its complexity.


\section{The Craft of Question Writing}
\label{sec:manchester:craft}

Trivia enthusiasts agree that questions need to be well written (despite other disagreements).
Asking ``good questions'' requires sophisticated pragmatic reasoning~\citep{hawkins-15}, and pedagogy explicitly acknowledges the complexity of writing effective questions for assessing student performance~\citep[focusing on multiple choice questions]{haladyna-04}.

\abr{qa} datasets, however, are often collected from the wild or written by untrained
crowdworkers.
Crowdworkers lack experience in 
crafting questions and may introduce idiosyncrasies that shortcut machine learning~\citep{geva-19}.  
Similarly, data collected from the wild
such as Natural Questions~\citep{kwiatkowski-19} or AmazonQA~\citep{gupta-19} by design have vast variations in quality.
In the previous section, we focused on how datasets as a whole should be structured.
Now, we focus on how specific \emph{questions} should be structured to make the dataset as valuable as possible.

\subsection{Avoiding superficial evaluations}

A related issue is that, in the words of \citet{voorhees-00}, ``there is no such
thing as a question with an obvious answer''.
As a consequence, trivia question authors 
delineate acceptable and unacceptable answers.
%
And when there's confusion, there's a process to adjudicate it.

This is again consistent with the theme of this chapter: how to make a mass market Turing Test.
%
In a real Turing test, if there's confusion about the question or its answer, you'd resolve it through careful negotiation (which would also show the intelligence of the answerer).
%
My favorite example of this Manchester paradigm exchange is when the
bridgekeeper---much like the Sphinx---asks travelers to answer questions or be
thrown into the abyss~\citep{monty-python-holy-grail}.
\begin{quote}\small
  \begin{tabular}{@{}p{0.22\textwidth}p{0.72\textwidth}@{}}
  Bridgekeeper: & What's the air-speed velocity of an unladen swallow? \\
  Arthur: & What do you mean? An African or European swallow? \\
  Bridgekeeper: & Huh? I don't know that! \\
  \end{tabular}
\end{quote}
Here, it's played for laughs as the questioner is shown to be unworthy and plunges to his death, but this dynamic shows that questions are not always perfect, and a smart answerer can show their own intelligence by questioning the question.

Unfortunately, the Cranfield paradigm's emphasis on \emph{reusable} collections---you can test the same property with many different systems and variants of a system---doesn't allow for nuanced discussions like that.
%
So the Manchester paradigm instead uses carefully crafted questions that avoid ambiguity (the original question should specify what kind of swallow) and also allow for all acceptable answers to be counted as correct.

This is in contrast to most \abr{ai} evaluations, where a desire to keep evaluations simple can produce misleading results by using either exact match or multiple choice evaluations.
%
Exact match is when an answer is viewed as correct when it \emph{exactly} matches something in the reference set of answers.
%
So if the reference answer is \answer{aluminium}, it would rule ``al'' (the chemical symbol), ``aluminum'' (the \abr{us} spelling), and ``alluminum'' (a misspelling) just as wrong as ``potato''.
%
Exact match consistently underestimates correctness in these settings~\citep{bulian2022tomayto}.
Open-domain \abr{qa} evaluation becomes more complicated once systems produce
free-form answers~\citep{kamalloo-etal-2023-evaluating}. More generally,
automatic open-\abr{qa} metrics can overestimate correctness on short or factual
answers~\citep{wang2023evaluating}.
Panel-based judges can make automatic evaluation more robust~\citep{verga2024replacingjudgesjuriesevaluating},
and trained judge models can make rubric-based evaluation cheaper to
apply~\citep{kim2024prometheus}; neither removes the need to define the
correctness rubric.
That is why importing Manchester-style rules is so useful: they give us a
shared definition of correctness even when the metric is imperfect.

There is a practical reason the community clings to exact match: it is fast,
transparent, and easy to reproduce. But it also bakes in a narrow view of
correctness that modern \abr{qa} systems regularly violate by producing valid
paraphrases or more specific answers.\footnote{There is increasingly
``\abr{llm}-as-a-judge'' for correctness evaluation, where an \abr{llm} is given
the answer key and the candidate answer and asked to make a
decision~\citep{zheng2023judging}. But automatic judges have their own failure
modes: length and style preferences~\citep{li2023alpacaeval},
self-preference~\citep{xu-etal-2024-pride}, demographic or positional
biases~\citep{ye2024justice}, and task-specific behavior that does not always
transfer cleanly~\citep{huang2024empirical}.
%
%We don't discuss them here in depth because we haven't covered the required models yet, but they can and should be guided by the kinds of Manchester guidelines we lay out here.
}

Multiple choice questions---where a question has a small set of 3--12 possible answers and the answerer needs to pick one correct answer---are another way to deal with this evaluation challenge.
%
We discuss multiple choice questions in more detail in Chapter~\ref{ch:leaderboards}, as they are an important part of the history of standardized tests.
%
Here, the designer of the question \textit{a priori} figures out what the correct and incorrect options are.
%
In addition to the obvious issue---there's a much higher chance of randomly guessing correctly---there are other subtler issues.
%
Sometimes the wrong answers give the game away. If one option is much more
specific, uses a different register, or is the only choice that matches the
dataset's usual answer style, a system may not need the question at all:
if you show only the choices but not the question, models can often still
answer the question~\citep{Balepur2024ArtifactsOA}. Partial-input probes expose
the same general problem in \abr{qa}: systems can exploit fragments of the input
instead of solving the intended task~\citep{richardson2020does}. Sometimes
the same item changes difficulty when the options move around; putting the
answer first instead of third should be irrelevant, but \abr{llm} predictions
can shift substantially under option reordering~\citep{pezeshkpour2023large}.

Of course, humans exploit the format too; there is a whole test-prep industry
to coach college applicants on how to ``ace the test''. A student might cross out two absurd
distractors, recognize a familiar-looking phrase in the remaining pair, and pick
correctly without being able to write the answer in free response~\citep{mckenna2019multiple}. Nor is a distractor always
cleanly wrong. Commonsense options can be plausible under a different reading of
the question, and users sometimes judge a distractor to be the most plausible
answer~\citep{Palta2024PlausiblyPQ}. Even when a model picks the right option,
it may not be able to explain why the other options are wrong~\citep{balepur-etal-2024-easy}.
%
It's possible to create better multiple choice questions---teachers have been writing them for decades---and I would argue that this is also part of the Manchester paradigm!
%
However, I don't want to talk too much about it here (even thought my student Nishant and I have written a series of papers in our crusade against evaluating \abr{ai}s with multiple choice evaluations) because I hope that multiple choice evaluations will fall away as we embrace the wiser evaluation strategies of the Manchester paradigm.

\section{How the Manchester Paradigm Deals with Unruely Answers}

For example, in writing for the trivia tournament Harvard Fall XI, Robert Chu uses a mental model of an answerer to explicitly delineate the range of acceptable correct answers:
\begin{quote}
     In Newtonian gravity, this quantity satisfies Poisson's equation. [\dots] For a dipole, this quantity is given by negative the dipole moment dotted with the electric field. [\dots] For 10 points, name this form of energy contrasted with kinetic.\\
    {\bf ANSWER:} \underline{potential energy} \textit{(prompt on energy; accept specific types like electrical potential energy or gravitational potential energy; do not accept or prompt on just ``potential'')}
\end{quote}

Likewise, the style guides for writing questions stipulate that you
must give the answer type clearly and early on.
These mentions specify whether you want a book, a collection, a movement, etc.  
It also signals the level of specificity requested.  
For example, a question about a date must state ``day and month required'' (\underline{September 11}, ``month and year required'' (\underline{April 1968}), or ``day, month, and year required'' (\underline{September 1, 1939}).
This is true for other answers as well: city and team, party and
country, or more generally ``two answers required''.
Despite these conventions, no pre-defined set of answers is perfect,
and every worthwhile trivia competition has a process for adjudicating
answers.



\abr{naqt} formalizes when aliases are acceptable, when specificity is
required, and when extra detail is disqualifying~\citep{naqt_correctness}.
\textit{Jeopardy!} has developed a similar case law for judging responses on
air~\citep{carberry_jeopardy_casebook}.
These rubrics make explicit the judgments that automated evaluations leave
implicit, and they highlight why correctness is about interpretation, not just
overlap.

\abr{naqt} is a thirty-year-old purveyor of \abr{qa} competitions spanning
middle schools to ``open'' competitions that anyone can join.
%
Because of the breadth of participants and judges (often drawn from parent
volunteers of participants), the organization has developed easy-to-follow
rules that make answer adjudication consistent (otherwise, participants would
complain about unfairness).
%
However, \abr{naqt} and \textit{Jeopardy!} formats are for
\emph{human} players, we cannot unquestioningly adopt the rules for machine
\abr{qa}.
%
For example, some rules cover in-person delivery of answers: mispronouncing
\underline{koan} as \underline{cone}, pauses between parts, etc.
%
These and other rules are irrelevant to text-based \abr{qa}.

%

Our goal is not to minimize the achievements of strong systems or the value of
quantitative evaluations. The point is more modest: \emph{blind automation} is
not enough to distinguish between systems on a leaderboard. If we want
evaluations that reward knowledge rather than artifacts, we need to borrow the
adjudication mindset of the trivia community: carefully written questions,
explicit answer rules, and a way to challenge mistakes.


This suggestion is not novel: \citet{voorhees-00} accept automatic
evaluations ``for experiments internal to an organization where the
benefits of a reusable test collection are most significant (\emph{and
  the limitations are likely to be understood})''~(our emphasis) but
that ``satisfactory techniques for [automatically] evaluating new
runs'' have not been found yet.  We are not aware of any change on
this front---if anything, we seem to have become more insensitive as a
community to just how limited our current evaluations are.

\section{The Questions that have Trained \abr{ai}}
\label{sec:manchester:forwardpoint}

The Manchester paradigm gives us a lens for reading the \abr{qa} datasets that
come next. We can ask whether a dataset is discriminative, whether it represents
real user needs, and whether its scoring rules reflect the kind of knowledge we
actually want systems to have.
%
Those questions will help us spot datasets that are the worst of both worlds:
not discriminative and not representative of real users' needs. They also point
to the opportunity for future research: build evaluations that keep the
reusability of Cranfield, the probing spirit of Turing, and the adjudication
norms of Manchester.
%
With that frame in place, we can turn to systems. The next chapter starts with
\textit{Watson}, an early state-of-the-art system that defeated Ken Jennings and
Brad Rutter on the American quiz show \jeopardy{}. Its success was one of the
first public harbingers of the \abr{ai} revolution, but it also shows why the
questions, scoring rules, and game format matter as much as the model itself.
