It also showed how ``good old-fashioned'' \abr{ai} systems use rules and logic to answer questions about specific domains.  But in \currentyear{}, ``\abr{ai} answering questions'' probably brings neural systems to mind: the systems that reply at the top of a search page or answer in a chat window.

In this chapter, I trace the recent history of neural question answering, from finding answers in documents to generating the kinds of answers people want.  This history mirrors the development of large language models: going from predicting the next word to learning to follow instructions. \section{Retrieving Evidence}

The general framework for answering a question has two steps: an
information retrieval system finds evidence, and a reader uses that
evidence to find the answer.

I now turn to how deep learning can improve the retrieval step.
The idea is called ``deep retrieval'': learn to turn both queries and
evidence into vectors that help find the answer.

Recall the sparse vectors with weights for individual words from
Section~\ref{sec:ir:tf-idf}.  For now, how those weights are set is
less important than the dimensionality and what kinds of things can
be matched: the weights could come from \tfidf{}, \abr{bm25}, or
just raw word counts.

Sparse word vectors can have tens or hundreds of thousands of dimensions.  English has many words, especially when a vocabulary includes rare names such as ``the Treaty of Westphalia'', ``Uluru'', and ``Murasaki''.  Sparse representations avoid storing a weight for every dimension, but the vocabulary still determines what can match.

Algorithms developed by Maryland's David Mount and his collaborators support approximate nearest-neighbor search~\citep{arya-98}.  Exact search is possible, but it can become expensive as the number of dimensions grows.

If searching these high-dimensional representations is expensive, why keep them around?  ``Min-Wise Independent Permutations''~\citep{broder-98} studies compact signatures for sets that let us estimate their similarity.  The broader idea of locality-sensitive hashing is to make similar objects more likely to share a hash value, so comparisons need not examine every object.

Min-wise hashing grew out of work on the search engine AltaVista.  I remember locality-sensitive hashing becoming popular when I was a graduate student; Moses Charikar was a professor at Princeton then.  These representations offered faster computations with less memory.

\section{A Vector in a Haystack}
\label{sec:methods:deep-retrieval}

Why bring up algorithms from the turn of the century in a discussion of neural retrieval?  Algorithms for comparing and searching vectors can also work with representations created by neural networks.  The goal is still to create representations suited to the task.

But what I really want to talk about is how these representations can be not just faster but can also be better!  Can we create representations tailor-made for a task like question answering?

Neural networks let us learn those representations.  But first, why might we want them?  Sometimes \tfidf{} is enough: ``Hall--H\'eroult process'' is a useful clue for finding information about smelting aluminum.  Other clues, such as ``Who said to be or not to be?'', consist of common words.  Adding $n$-gram dimensions could help, but it would expand the vocabulary even further.

Even worse, sometimes people asking questions, particularly in the Manchester paradigm (Chapter~\ref{ch:manchester}), can be tricky!  If the question is about Luebeck, a city that was part of the Hanseatic League, the question writer might be tricky and say something like ``a city on the Trave that was a member of a confederation of Baltic cities'' instead of mentioning the Hanseatic League by name.

A neural network can learn representations for both questions and evidence passages.  Training pushes ``Who said to be or not to be?'' toward passages containing ``Hamlet'', and the clue about the Trave toward passages about Luebeck.  Frameworks such as PyTorch make it possible to train both encoders together; \abr{bert}~\citep{devlin-19} is one choice for the encoder.

Back in 2015, my collaborators and I used deep averaging networks to represent \quizbowl{} questions~\citep{iyyer-15}.  In a two-dimensional projection of questions about \abr{us} presidents, the representations progress clockwise from the founding fathers through the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.
% AUTHOR: Restore the presidential-embedding projection from the lecture slides.

The dot product from Equation~\ref{eq:ir:dot-product} also scores learned
question and passage vectors in \abr{dpr}~\citep{karpukhin-20}; inside a
Transformer, attention uses scaled query--key dot products, converts them
to weights with a softmax, and takes a weighted sum of the corresponding
value vectors~\citep{vaswani-17}.

In 2019 and 2020, three papers developed neural retrieval for question answering: \abr{orqa} (Open-Retrieval Question Answering)~\citep{lee-19}, \abr{realm} (Retrieval-Augmented Language Model Pre-Training)~\citep{guu-20}, and \abr{dpr} (Dense Passage Retrieval)~\citep{karpukhin-20}.

\abr{orqa} shows that learned retrieval can beat an off-the-shelf retrieval system on questions from users seeking information, although \abr{bm25} remains competitive when question writers already know the answer.

\abr{realm} changes pretraining to mask spans likely to be answers.  Where \abr{bert} selects 15\% of tokens for its masked-language-model objective, \abr{realm} targets named entities and dates.  Consider this passage: ``During this conflict, Celia S\'anchez created a `farmer's militia' in order to supply troops, and worked with Frank Pa\'is in order to create a task force.''

This could be part of a \quizbowl{} question or a Wikipedia passage.  For pretraining, it is simply raw text.  Masking the names makes the model recover the kinds of information questions often ask for: people, places, organizations, and dates.

\abr{dpr} also examines how to choose negative training examples: passages that should not be retrieved for a question.

Suppose a training batch contains four questions, each with a positive passage.  For the second question, its own passage is the positive example, while the other questions' passages provide in-batch negatives.  This reuses the passages already encoded for the batch.

\abr{dpr} also uses hard negatives: passages that a \abr{bm25} retriever ranks highly but that do not contain the answer.

These hard negatives improve retrieval in the experiments reported by \citet{karpukhin-20}.

These models learn to connect questions with evidence even when their words differ.  I think there is still plenty to explore in what those representations can capture.

How does dense retrieval fit with extracting answer spans?  Instead of indexing entire documents, the retriever can encode passages, such as Wikipedia paragraphs, that a reader can examine for an answer (Section~\ref{sec:methods:reading}).

The same kind of encoder can therefore support both steps: retrieving a promising passage and locating an answer within it.

Dense retrieval also opens up other kinds of input, which I return to in Section~\ref{sec:methods:multimodal}.

Dense passage retrieval lets you find the answer to a question like ``how was the monkey king born''.  But since we're doing the lookup in an arbitrary vector space, we could train our question encoder to take input in any language: all that matters is that the resulting vector space lines up.  But then why does it even have to be text?  We can encode any object as a vector, so why not answer questions with an image?

I started with the cost of representing a large vocabulary and ended with representations that could connect text and images.  So perhaps a picture encoding is worth a thousand words.

\section{Machine Reading: Extracting Answers from Text}
\label{sec:methods:reading}

Machine reading uses retrieved evidence to find an answer.  Natural Questions~\citep{kwiatkowski-19}, introduced in Section~\ref{sec:datasets:nq}, supplies questions, Wikipedia pages, and annotated answers.
Consider these three versions of the same question:
\begin{itemize}
\item ``when was the name windsor adopted by the royal family'' --- \answer{1917}
\item ``when did the royal family take the name windsor'' --- \answer{1917}
\item ``when did the royal family adopt the name windsor'' --- \answer{in 1917}
\end{itemize}

The retrieval step in Section~\ref{sec:methods:deep-retrieval} provides passages for the reader to examine.

Natural Questions provides Wikipedia pages found through Google's search engine.  An open-domain system must retrieve its own evidence, but the reader's job remains the same: find an answer in the passages it receives.

An extractive reader works much like a highlighter: given a question and a passage, it marks where an answer starts and ends.\footnote{It can also abstain; deciding whether to answer matters for buzzing in Section~\ref{sec:watson:strategy}.}

Annotated spans provide training data: the reader learns to assign high probability to answer spans and low probability to other spans.  As my collaborators and I discuss in the \abr{pedants} paper~\citep{li-etal-2024-pedants}, this is usually fairly easy, but it can get complicated.  Let's just review a few monarchs to make the point.  For example, is Elizabeth I the same person as Elizabeth II?  No.  Is James II the same person as James VII?  Uh, they could be \dots{} since James II of England was James VII of Scotland.    Is Catherine the Great the same person as Charles the Bald?  No, of course not.  Is Charlemagne the same person as Karl der Grosse?  Yes, depending on whether you ask a French or a German \dots{} and don't dare ask them whether that monarch who founded the Holy Roman Empire had his octagonal palace in Aachen or Aix-la-Chapelle.

So let's go back to the Windsor questions.  We know that the answer is \answer{1917}, so a string-matching procedure could highlight every occurrence of that year in a retrieved passage, including this one from Wikipedia's article on the House of Windsor:

\evidence{High anti-German sentiment amongst the people of the British Empire during the First World War reached a peak in March \answer{1917}, when the Gotha G.IV, a heavy aircraft capable of crossing the English Channel, began bombing London directly and became a household name.}\footnote{\url{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Windsor}; emphasis added.}

The highlighted year matches the answer string, but the passage describes bombing, not the royal family's adoption of the name Windsor.  A system could therefore return the right string for the wrong reason.  The hope is that training gives higher scores to spans supported by the surrounding evidence and lower scores to these spurious matches.\footnote{But what if there is no good answer?  A reader may return the best-looking span anyway, answering a question such as ``When did Leonardo da Vinci paint the Sistine Chapel?'' with ``between 1508 and 1512''.}

As Section~\ref{sec:methods:preferences} discusses, people can prefer different answers to the same question---but for a while, this seemed to work fairly well.  Google would ``call out'' the answers to obvious questions without forcing you to click through to the underlying page, and it used its world-class information retrieval to find a page that might contain the answer, extract a span with that answer from the page, and then provide that answer.  Going back to our running example, a system would score all the start and stop positions, and we could rank all of the possibilities in order of their scores.

Naively you could just take the top answer, or you could look at the top answers and decide whether this set was something you could be proud of.  For example, if there were two high-scoring contradictory answers, you might not choose to answer at all.  This is basically where we were around 2020.  As anyone who has gone down a Wikipedia hole knows, sometimes you need several pieces of information to fully answer a question.

\section{Following Multiple Pieces of Evidence}
\label{sec:methods:multihop}

Section~\ref{sec:formats:multihop} introduced questions that require multiple pieces of evidence; here I work through how a reader can connect them.

Let's work through trying to solve them, using Wikipedia paragraphs as our source documents.  And hopefully this will show you how hard the problem is.

Consider a Lorenzo Lotto clue.  The question is: ``A Lorenzo Lotto painting of this woman includes a cartouche showing her response to an accusation.''  Okay, so let's see what turns up with a search of Wikipedia.  ``Cartouche'' is a very high \tfidf{} word, so let's see what Wikipedia has to say about Lotto and cartouches:
\begin{itemize}
\item \evidence{The San Giacomo dell'Orio Altarpiece is signed and dated on the cartouche hanging at the throne's base.}

\item \evidence{The character at the left of Jesus is a true caricature\dots{} The man in the lower left corner has a cartouche on his beret, a custom of the Pharisees.}

\item \evidence{Lotto portrayed her after getting naked for the bath\dots{} The cartouches, showing the characters' dialogue, are an archaic element}

\end{itemize}

The first step is to figure out which one of these is correct.  In the first evidence, you need to know that a signature and a date aren't a response to an accusation, so that's out.  Then you need to know that a cartouche on ``his'' beret is inconsistent with the question's use of ``this woman'', so that just leaves the last piece of evidence, where you have a woman and dialog in a cartouche.  This is why the distractor setting in HotpotQA can be so tricky!  A search result can turn up a bunch of seemingly relevant material, but you need to pick the right answer out of it somehow.  What makes the distractor setting of HotpotQA more tractable than \quizbowl{} is that the answer is in there \emph{somewhere} which is not guaranteed in \quizbowl{}.  Moreover, in \quizbowl{} the correct pieces of evidence are not annotated.

Okay, now the challenge is to figure out who this ``her'' is here.  So if you go to the start of this article, you can see that this is Susanna from the Book of Daniel.  So often, this multihop reasoning is essentially coreference resolution either within a document or across documents.

So how can this be across documents?  That's not how pronouns work, you might say!  To show you what I mean, let's take a look at a \quizbowl{} example that does require knowing who entities are across documents:

\question{A mechanic was arrested in one of these two countries for using women's underwear soaked in invisible ink to pass secrets to the other.}

We need to figure out what these two countries are.  So let's do a query.  So we get this pretty unambiguous result:

\evidence{He was later accused in Cairo of using women's underwear soaked in invisible ink to pass information to Mossad.}

But we don't know the answer, which needs to be two countries!  What's this Mossad thing?  And is Cairo a country?  If it isn't we need to figure out what country it's in.  So perhaps you as a human know the answer, but a computer needs to keep hopping.  So let's get a couple more hops to figure out what countries are being referenced here.  Then we find out that Cairo is the capital of Egypt and that Mossad is the CIA, MI6, BND, what have you\dots{} equivalent for Israel.

\subsection{Learning to Find the Next Hop}

How do systems actually gather the evidence for these multihop questions?  As you can imagine, this requires finding multiple pieces of evidence.  Let’s first talk about different strategies for gathering the evidence: you can follow structured links, you can generate real text queries, or you can generate vector-based queries.

Let’s first talk about following structured links.  This is probably the most obvious and is probably the most similar to what you’d do if you as a human typed in a query, found a page, and then clicked on the link that got you a little bit closer to what you thought the answer would be.  Of course, knowing which link to explore takes a little trial and error.  We did something like this in 2020 with a model called DELFT, and this paper from Chen et al. explores different ways of following links: following a link on the Wikipedia page or resolving a coreference chain … which is a lot like the examples that we showed in Section~\ref{sec:methods:multihop} (for example, getting the answer to Susanah and the Elders, requires figuring out who “her” is).  Then my former student Chen Zhao followed up our work with a collaboration with Microsoft called TransformerXH.  This seems like the most popular approach.

But sometimes you can’t find a link that easily.  There could be a different strategy that you could use to solve a multihop question.  If you typed into the Wikipedia search bar to get the first query, why not just type a new query?  This is the approach taken by Qi et al with the wonderfully named model “Golden Retriever”.  It learns how to formulate queries \emph{conditioned} on the original query, evidence you got from the first query, to get to the next piece of evidence.  For example here, to solve this question it explicitly creates the query “Armada author” to figure out that it was written by Ernest Cline.

One nice thing this about this is that these approaches are pretty interpretable: knowing what searches and evidence led to the answer lets a user inspect the chain to know if it’s good or not.  This paper from Inoue et al. goes even further by doing abstractive summarization to create sentences that find the minimal information that’s sufficient for answering the question.  So then for the question “Charlie Rowe plays Billy Costa in a film based on what novel?” you get this nice abstracted explainer that says “Charlie Rowe plays Billy Costa in the Golden Compass.  The Golden Compass is a film based on the novel Northern Lights.”  Pretty good!

But do you actually need to put your fingers on the keyboard to get to the evidence?  We saw with DPR that you can actually do better with vector-based queries.  So why create an explicit string when it’s just going to get turned into a vector anyway?  That’s the approach of Xiong et al who just directly optimize these vector queries.

The challenge is that all of these approaches need to be trained somehow.  Like for any machine learning problem, you need training data.  Existing techniques for solving this use the explicit correct hops from datasets like MultiHop.  But could we do this in datasets like quiz bowl were the evidence is not annotated?  

Let’s think this through and see where it breaks down.  You take your original question, turn it into a vector, get some search results, and then from that you generate a new vector query and you get some results.  

The first challenge is how do you know if a piece of evidence is good or not?  This is something that my former student Chen Zhao looked at in an EMNLP paper from 2021.

The Windsor example in Section~\ref{sec:methods:reading} showed why containing the answer string is not enough: the passage must support the answer to this question.

Let’s go over this with another example.  Let’s say we want to answer the question “What state does Sang-Wook Cheong work as a materials scientist?”.  We get our second hop query after the first hop says he works at Rutgers.  The second hop needs to tell us the Rutgers is in New Jersey.  We can always say that everything without “New Jersey” isn’t a good piece of evidence.  In addition, anything that has the “New Jersey” but doesn’t actually answer the question isn’t a good piece of evidence: like this thing about Fountains of Wayne, which is also in New Jersey.  And things that answer the question and look like an answer to the question we keep.

This now forms a cycle: start with \emph{some} IR-based QA model, gather some evidence, label it as we just talked about, and retrain a reader, and more importantly a retriever.  Then start the cycle again.  And you can also train a reranker to squeeze out a few more points.

So let’s see if this actually works.  Compared to traditional IR, trained models that use the explicit evidence from HotPotQA for fully supervised training like Transformer XH from MSR can do much better, getting the answer in the top 10 results 90\% of the time.  We can get almost the same results without those annotations, suggesting our approach not just works, but it needs strictly less data.

Nonetheless, it’s still well short of the creativity and the depth that motivated, smart humans can.  In other words, it still well short of our goal of automating Dan Russell.  And even within the limited world of HotPotQA, there’s some recent work showing that models are taking shortcuts to get to the answer and not creating complete reasoning chains.

Jiang and Bansal show that there are reasoning shortcuts that models can take.  For example, in this document, if you have the question “What was the father of Kasper Schmeichel voted to be by the IFFHS in 1992”.  First, let’s take a moment to stare at the odd phrasing: you wouldn’t get this in a Manchester paradigm question.  But you can grab the answer just in a single document.  Jiang and Bansal create new documents that force true multihop reasoning: I’d rather create better questions rather than manufacture evidence to make the problem harder.  But nonetheless, it shows the limitations of HotPotQA.

Similarly, Trivedi et al. create a metric called “DiRe” for “Disconnected Reasoning” to measure whether a system gets the wrong answer when you remove relevant evidence.  A good MultiHop dataset should be difficult enough to not allow this sort of cheating.  In other words, this is a metric to show how often the issues that Jiang and Bansal expose show up.

All of these open questions make multihop question answering one of the most exciting areas for QA research at the moment.  Because the answers are still short (mostly factoid), it doesn’t have the same issues that make long form QA evaluation so fiendishly difficult, but the modeling challenges are still real.  And now that there are approaches that get beyond the requirements of gold evidence, we can move beyond datasets with gold evidence labels.

And I think we have a lot to learn from humans here—explicitly incorporating human strategies for solving multihop challenges to synthesize vector queries, following links, or synthesizing raw text queries.

And this can be paired with some of the fancy machine learning tools that we have like reinforcement learning for uncovering the best actions given sparse feedback … could this get us better models from more complicated but more lightly annotated datasets?

And then perhaps we better address the “Joy of Search” problems in a hybrid cooperation between humans and computers: where computers and humans could join hands and hop together.

\section{From Open Books to Closed Books}
\label{sec:methods:generation}
But around 2020, we started moving away from ``open book'' exams, where a computer had to find the answer in Wikipedia to ``closed book'' exams where the models had to memorize the answers.  This was a big change: In SQuAD, you get the passage in front of you with the answer in front of you.  You just need to highlight it.  Open domain \abr{qa} with machine reading is a little bit harder, but you need to find the answer in Wikipedia.  You still have access to Wikipedia.

Is that fair?  We don't let humans bring all of Wikipedia in when they take the SATs.  One thing my collaborators and I did in the EfficientQA competition~\citep{min2021efficientqa} was try to level the playing field by limiting the amount of information the systems could store.  For example, one track limited the overall system to 6\,GB.  Many systems did some sort of pruning for an open domain \abr{qa} system.  But is that the most efficient way to do it?  Perhaps you could just store what you need in the model itself.  Would that be more efficient?

Large language models can store information in their parameters.  T5~\citep{raffel-20}, developed at Google, casts many language tasks, including question answering, as generating text from text.
T5's pretraining objective resembles \abr{bert}'s, but it reconstructs missing spans of text rather than predicting each masked token separately.  These spans become multiword ``slots'' to fill.

And this makes a lot of sense!  Think about what happens when you have a question on, say, \jeopardyf{}  As Chapter~\ref{ch:watson} explains, the first step is to find the lexical answer type.  So in some sense you're filling in the blank: In 2001, \underline{\hspace{2cm}} became the shortest \abr{nba} \abr{mvp} in history.  But you have some additional information: that blank needs to be a person, a guard, and a member of the Philadelphia 76ers.

That resembles T5's pretraining task: recover the missing text.  In this example, the missing name is Allen Iverson.

The first reason to prefer retrieval-based open book \abr{qa} is that if you trust the underlying source (like Wikipedia), then you can trust the answer more.  A human can inspect the source and verify that it does indeed answer the question.  In contrast, large language models sometimes just ``make stuff up'' (``hallucination'' in the lingo, although Geoff Hinton prefers ``confabulation'') and there's no easy way to check if there's any evidence to support it.

And here's where I can't back up my guess, but I think that generation will struggle with less common answers.  If you have a large enough pool of documents, you can focus your model on generalization rather than memorizing the long tail of millions of possible answers.

But that large pool of documents in open book \abr{qa} comes at a cost!  The closed book assumption can be much more space efficient.  But these models, as we've seen, can do more than just answer one-off questions.

\section{Long Answers}
\label{sec:methods:long-answers}

For shorter answers, we've been assuming that you can figure out whether two answers are equivalent.  This becomes much more difficult for longer answers.
Section~\ref{sec:methods:reading} considered this problem for names; long answers introduce many more ways to express the same information.
Just because you answer differently from the reference doesn't mean that it's wrong.  Let's start with Apu Nahasapeemapetilon's answer to ``What was the cause of the \abr{us} Civil War?''

\begin{quote}
\textbf{Examiner:} All right, here's your last question. What was the cause of the Civil War?

\textbf{Apu:} Actually, there were numerous causes. Aside from the obvious schism between the abolitionists and the anti-abolitionists, there were economic factors, both domestic and inter...

\textbf{Examiner:} Wait, wait... just say slavery.

\textbf{Apu:} Slavery it is, sir.
\end{quote}

% AUTHOR: Confirm the sources for the Dagan and Socher AI-completeness attributions below.
Like the examiner, an evaluator may prefer a shorter answer because it is easier to score.   There are many ways to answer ``What was the cause of the American Civil War''.  Everything that Apu said was correct, and there are many ways to phrase what he said.  Detecting all the things that are semantically equivalent to that is itself an \abr{ai}-complete problem, as Ido Dagan argues.\footnote{And Richard Socher argued the same thing about question answering.}  As the discussion of \abr{eli5} in Section~\ref{sec:formats:long-answers} illustrates, we have examples of what makes for a good answer if the answers are supposed to be long: e.g., someone masterfully ``explaining like I'm five'' \question{why things go bad more quickly when you open a food container}.

% https://www.reddit.com/r/explainlikeimfive/comments/1fumo1i/eli5_how_do_things_expire_once_you_open_them/?share_id=i52WEl4Yg5-56USkz0EhS&utm_content=1&utm_medium=android_app&utm_name=androidcss&utm_source=share&utm_term=1

A computer need not recreate the reference answer to give a good answer.  So how do you tell whether its output is good?
We have examples of questions and good answers, but string equality cannot tell us whether a different answer is also good.
Machine translation faces a similar problem: many translations can express the same meaning.  \citet{papineni-02} propose \abr{bleu}, Bilingual Evaluation Understudy, which uses modified $n$-gram precision and a brevity penalty to compare system outputs with reference translations.

The ``understudy'' stands in for human judgments of translation quality.  For comparing answers, a related metric is \abr{rouge}, introduced by \citet{lin-04}.  While \abr{bleu} means ``blue'' in French, \abr{rouge} means ``red'' and stands for Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation.  Its recall measures ask how much of the reference's wording appears in the candidate.

Like \abr{bleu}, \abr{rouge} looks for a certain overlap in the words.  But you don't want to just treat this as a bag of words: the order matters for conveying the same information.  So instead they look for overlap in things called n-grams, or a sequence of contiguous words.  Let's start with bigrams for simplicity: we're going to look for all pairs of consecutive words that overlap.

This is clearer with an example, so let's take an example of a longer answer to the question \question{what is best in life}?  In the film Conan the Barbarian,\footnote{I've edited these for the purposes of the example.  Bonus points if you remember the exact quote.} a candidate answer was \answer{Fleet horses beneath you, the open steppe before you, and falcons and wind coursing through the heavens.}  However, Conan contends that the correct answer is \answer{To crush the enemy's army and fleet, see them driven before you, and to hear their lamentations wind through the air}.  The question is: how good of an answer was our candidate answer?

\abr{rouge}-2 counts adjacent pairs of words: there are twenty such bigrams in the reference answer.\footnote{As Section~\ref{sec:ir:tf-idf} discusses, tokenization matters; here I lowercase the words, ignore punctuation, and keep possessives as one token.}  Of those twenty bigrams, three appear in the candidate: ``before you'', ``you and'', and ``through the''.  The recall is therefore $3/20=0.15$.  But this ignores order beyond each bigram and requires you to pick how many words you want to consider, so another alternative is what's called \abr{rouge}-L, which looks at the longest subsequence that matches between the candidate and the reference.

The longest subsequence is the longest chain of words that are in the reference and the candidate that appear in the same order.  In this case this would be: ``fleet\dots before you and \dots wind \dots through the'': a total of seven words.  Recall precision and recall from Section~\ref{sec:ir:precision-recall}.  We compute the precision---of what we found, how much was right---and the recall---of what was right, how much did we find---for the longest common subsequence as well.  The candidate had seventeen words, so precision is $\frac{7}{17}$.  Likewise, the reference had twenty-one words, so the recall is $\frac{7}{21}=\frac{1}{3}$.  These two numbers are combined into a single score; higher is better.\footnote{Typically by a weighted harmonic mean.}  Of course, there are many problems with this: ``fleet'' is a homonym, not the same word, ``the'' counts as much as ``wind''.  But it's a place to start.\footnote{One nice thing about this is that it's upper bounded by the unigram overlap between the two strings.}

However, \abr{rouge} can reward the wrong things.  \citet{krishna-21} show that a system can score well on \abr{eli5} without grounding its answers in retrieved evidence.  Repeating the question or copying a training answer can also earn misleadingly high scores.  Their analysis finds substantial overlap between training and validation questions, so memorization can masquerade as generalization.

\section{Learning Which Answers People Prefer}
\label{sec:methods:preferences}

This makes it hard to prove that your snazzy new algorithm is actually good.  So what's the answer? Asking people what they think is the better answer.  This ends up being how we trained modern \abr{ai} systems, so let's talk about how they work a little bit more.  Building a modern \abr{ai} system roughly has two phases: pretraining and posttraining.  In pretraining, you are learning to output the next word.  Mathematically, this is very similar to the logistic regression system that we talked about in Section~\ref{sec:watson:logistic-regression}.  The difference is that the inputs to the systems are the embeddings that we talked about in Section~\ref{sec:methods:deep-retrieval}: vectors that are learned to best represent the word in context.

But just predicting the next word is not all that useful for these models: it doesn't actually follow instructions.  If you say something like ``List of presidents who were born in Virginia'', an algorithm that just predicts the next word would do things like ``List of presidents who were born in New York''.  While as we discuss in Chapter~\ref{ch:watson}, you can form questions into ``fill in the blank'' settings like the \jeopardy{}-style questions we covered earlier, it doesn't work for more complicated questions.

So we need to train the models to answer long-form questions better.  Let's say that we have examples of model outputs.  So let's say that we have three outputs: a human prefers 1 over 2 over 3.  We can then look at all pairs of examples: 1 is better than 2, 2 is better than 3, 1 is better than 3.  For all of the pairs, we have one winner and one loser.

We want the policy to produce answers people prefer.  To learn those preferences, a reward model assigns a score to each answer, and training encourages it to score the preferred answer above the rejected answer.

A logistic function turns the difference between the two reward scores into a preference probability.  Recall its shape from Figure~\ref{fig:watson:sigmoid}: it gives an output between zero and one, and an input of zero gives 0.5.

If the preferred answer's score is much higher, that probability approaches one; if its score is much lower, the probability approaches zero.  This is an old idea: reinforcement-learning papers often call it the Bradley--Terry model.

David Hunter reviews this history~\citep{hunter-bradley-terry-04}, but this goes back at least to Zermelo in the twenties, and it's been rediscovered in multiple fields.  The basic idea is that there are two things that are being compared on some scalar continuum and you need to decide yes/no or which is better.  If you work through the math, you end up putting it through a logistic function: comparing your skill to a question, comparing a legislator's ideology to a piece of proposed legislation, or comparing two chess players.  Or in this case, two responses from a model!

Anyhow, now that we have this reward function that captures the preferences from humans, we still have another problem: we don't want our policy to \emph{just} chase these preferences.  We still want it to be a good language model.  So the objective that we actually optimize is: maximizing the learned reward minus a penalty that prevents us from getting too far away from the original language model.

In that objective, $\lambda$ is a parameter that says how important it is to stay close to the old language model.  If it's zero, we just chase the preference reward.  The bigger it is, the less important the preference information is.

For intuition, think about the $D_{\mathrm{KL}}$ term as a distance function: it's zero if these two things are the same, it gets bigger if they're not.  Again, there are some details here that we won't get into.  This is because these policies are language models.  Their output is a distribution over language.  So you need a comparison over distributions, so this is actually an information-theoretic divergence called the Kullback--Leibler divergence (hence the \abr{kl}).  It measures divergence between probability distributions rather than distance between points.

There is still a practical problem: we cannot ask a human to rate every answer generated during training.  In reinforcement learning from human feedback~\citep{ouyang-22}, a learned reward model estimates human preferences for new outputs, while a value function helps estimate expected future reward during policy training.  This lets training use human judgments beyond the particular answers people rated.

\section{But That Assumes a Good Question}
\label{sec:methods:presuppositions}

All of this breaks down, however, when the question is problematic.  For example, some questions that tripped up early \abr{qa} systems were ``When did Leonardo paint the Sistine Chapel'' or ``Is the king of France bald''.  These questions contain what are called ``false presuppositions'': Michelangelo painted the Sistine Chapel, and France no longer has a king.  Because a reward model can learn what looks like a good answer to familiar questions, and those questions look like legitimate questions, it can encourage answers that accept the false presuppositions and blithely answer.

You might think that these questions are unrealistic, but questions with hidden assumptions are common in the medical domain.
For example, \citet{srikanth-24-pregnant} examine the question ``Can I breastfeed my baby when I am ill?''  A model can go along with a question's assumptions---like an improv actor saying ``yes and''~\citep{cho-20-spolin}---but a helpful answer may need to challenge them.  In the paper's example, the fuller answer addresses the belief that all illnesses pass through breast milk and explains that antibodies in breast milk can help protect the baby.

But how do you push back?  This requires coming up with a strategy.  Sometimes you're missing information.  If the question is ``what's the capital of Georgia'', you might need to ask which Georgia they mean.  You might need to ask for context: ``how old is the baby'', ``what were you sick with''.  These are called discourse moves, and they direct the conversation in different directions.  \citet{srikanth-26-discotrace} show how different communities favor different answering strategies, including clarifying a question or challenging its assumptions.

Given this, how do you get to a good answer?

\section{Planning a Good Answer}
\label{sec:methods:planning}

Pushing back against a flawed question isn't the only case where you need to plan before you answer.  Generating intermediate reasoning is often called ``chain of thought''~\citep{wei-22}, and it can build the scaffolding of an answer before you get the true answer.  For example, this can help address the multihop questions we saw in Section~\ref{sec:formats:multihop}.

For the Egypt/Israel question in Section~\ref{sec:methods:multihop}, an intermediate scratchpad could connect the evidence before giving the final answer:
\begin{quote}
To answer this question, I need to figure out both countries.  Mossad is associated with Israel, and the arrest took place in Cairo, which is in Egypt.  These two pieces of evidence point to ``Israel and Egypt''.
\end{quote}

\section{But What About Non-Text?}
\label{sec:methods:multimodal}

Thus far, all of the questions we've talked about in this chapter have been text.  But most of the methods naturally extend to other modalities.  As we discussed in Section~\ref{sec:formats:multimodal}, multimodal encoders can transform multiple languages, images, and audio into a consistent representation.  Those become the same kind of input to the models that predict the next word.  Those models can answer questions directly or be ``fine-tuned'' to mimic better answers or fine-tuned to produce chain of thought analysis before their final answer.
