ubiquitous portals to the Internet---\textit{Bing}, \textit{Yahoo!},
\textit{Baidu}, or \textit{Ask Jeeves}---seemed like a fundamental law of science.
%
With the onslaught of \abr{ai}, they almost seem antiquated\dots my
parents use \textit{Google}, but I use Grok.
%
But it wasn’t always like that.  Just as there was a time before \abr{ai}, there was 
also a time before search
engines, and although there’s a certain charm to card catalogs, that’s
not an effective way to find information.
%
But that was essentially the world before search engines: you had to
find what you wanted from a list or index that a human (usually a librarian) painstaikingly curated for you.

This chapter will give that history 
and how it made the twentieth century Internet possible.
%
That's a bigger story, but for the purposes of this book about question answering, 
this is one of the first times that scientifically struggled with the question 
of taking a question like \question{who is on first?} and algorithmically deciding if it

%
A shorthand for this struggle and the new frontiers of information science
that it opened up is called the \textit{Cranfield Paradigm}.

\section{Cleverdon's Clever Collection}
\label{sec:ir:cranfield}

The story of the Cranfield Paradigm begins in 1967 with Cyril Cleverdon at Cranfield University
in England: which has an airport right on campus.\footnote{My students
at the University of Maryland have to walk a whole fifteen minutes
to get to the oldest continuously operated airport in the world.}
%
The University was the heart of British aviation research.
%
Cyril Cleverdon was in charge of their library there.  And he was
into computers.  He wanted to see if he could measure how good the answers provided by an index were.

\begin{figure}
  \includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/external/Cranfield_Airport_by_Thomas_Nugent}

  \caption{Photo of Cranfield University (and its adjacent airstrip).
    Photo by Thomas Nugent [Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike
    2.0 license].}
\end{figure}

Now, when I say ``index'', if you're over 40, you might think of this
sort of thing in the back of a book~\citep{ritchie88}:
\begin{center}
  \begin{tabular*}{0.9\linewidth}{@{}l@{\extracolsep{\fill}}r@{}}
    \multicolumn{2}{r@{}}{INDEX 269} \\[\smallskipamount]
    \texttt{read} system call & 170 \\
    \texttt{readdir} function & 170 \\
    \texttt{readlines} function & 170 \\
    \texttt{realloc} library function & 170 \\
    recursion & 86, 139, 141, 182, 269
  \end{tabular*}
\end{center}
An author carefully wrote down topics mentioned in a text and told you
where you could find them (i.e., which page of the book they were
on).\footnote{In this case, they also helpfully told you that
  ``recursion'' appeared in the index (the page you're looking at), a
  computer science joke.}

But if you’re younger and a computer scientist, you probably think of
an index as a lookup table of every darn word possible.
%
The reason you think that is because of the Cranfield experiments.
%
His claim, which was quite controversial at the time, was that you
could look up documents based on the words in the document.
%
You didn’t need laboriously curated indices you could use a machine,
an engine if you will, to search through them.
%
The claim was that the Universal Decimal Classification, a facet
classification, and alphabetical subject catalog, and a unigram index
were roughly comparable~\citep{CLEVERDON-67}.

Before we analyze this, just a reminder of how a search engine query
is evaluated. 
%
A user walks up to a terminal in the library, types in a query like\footnote{At the time, comptuers couldn't process grammatical questions; later chapters will discuss how we overcame that hurdle.}
\question{Vaudeville Comedy Baseball}.
%
Then, it provided some results.  The search worked if most of the results were \textbf{relevant}: i.e., the user saw those results and said, ``yes, that's what I'm looking for''.

This might seem like old hat to you today, but it was revolutionary at the
time.  To evaluate a system’s ability to search a dataset, you take
some queries from users and then find the truly relevant documents to
that query.  Then, given a system’s results, you compute how well the system
delivered those relevant results to a user.

\section{Is it Better to Find All the Good Things or for Everything Found to be Good?}
\label{sec:ir:precision-recall}

There are two ways to measure the quality of those results: precision and recall~\citet{keen-66}.
%
But to fully define these concepts, let's get a little bit more formal and provide sizes to the stuff our system found.
%
First let's consider---given a query---how many answers you
retrieved.
%
Some of those were relevant to the query and gave you information you
wanted.  Let's say there are $A$ of those.  But others were not
relevant, these are distractions that you still have to wade
through.  Let's say there are $B$ of those.

\begin{table}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{lccc}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Relevant} & \textbf{Non-relevant} & \textbf{Total} \\
\midrule
\textbf{Retrieved} 
    & $A_q$ 
    & $B_q$ 
    & $A_q + B_q$ \\
\textbf{Not Retrieved} 
    & $C_q$ 
    & $D_q$ 
    & $C_q + D_q$ \\
\midrule
\textbf{Total} 
    & $A_q + C_q$ 
    & $B_q + D_q$ 
    & $A_q + B_q + C_q + D_q$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Contingency table for question $q$ used to define precision
  and recall.}
\end{table}

Clearly it's better to have $A$ be higher than $B$, and this metric is
called \emph{precision}:
\begin{equation}
  \mbox{precision}(q) = \frac{A_q}{A_q + B_q},
\end{equation}
of the answers you provided, how many were relevant.  The higher the
better.
%
But let's consider how many of the relevant answers you did find:
$A_q$.  It would be better if $A_q$ were \emph{all} the relevant
documents, of which there are $A_q$ and $C_q$.
%
This is called the recall:
\begin{equation}
  \mbox{recall}(q) = \frac{A_q}{A_q + C_q}.
\end{equation}
Both of these numbers are between 0 (bad) and 1 (good); ideally, you
would want both to be 1.0, but this is hard to achieve.
%
You can get perfect recall by returning all possible answers, but then
your precision sinks.
%
If you return only the answers you're absolutely certain of, your
recall suffers.

A good score depends on what the user needs from the answer.
Different applications (or users) would have different tradeoffs.
%
If you're trying to find a ``smoking gun'' in a court case, you want
recall over all else: it doesn't matter if you have to comb through
too many irrelevant documents.
%
But if you're trying to answer a simple question, you don't need every
document that tells you what the capital of Arkansas is, you just need
one, and you don't want anything irrelevant.

Sometimes you want to balance precision and recall.  A common
combination of these two measures of goodness is $F_1$ score, which is
the harmonic mean of precision and recall:
\begin{equation}
  F_1(q) = 2 \frac{\operatorname{precision}(q) \cdot
    \operatorname{recall}(q)}{\operatorname{precision}(q) + \operatorname{recall}(q)}.
\label{eq:f-score}
\end{equation}

So, if you have a set of queries $q_1$\dots $q_N$ you can run your
system on each question, get a set of results, compute the precision
and recall of each one, and then create a ranking of the systems.

Let’s pause for a moment to recognize how revolutionary this was.
%
Once you found the relevant documents, you can evaluate \emph{any}
system.
%
Before, you had to run an expensive user experiment every time you
tweaked your system: did that output look good?
%
How about this one?
%
With a reusable test collection, you could turn the crank on your
retrieval system without pesky humans getting in the way.  Just check
whether the red box finds the relevant documents!

But not everybody was convinced.  The Cranfield paradigm is making a lot of assumptions here: 
first, if a document is relevant, it’s equally relevant: everything is equally relevant: showing you my explanation of topic modeling is as good as a video from Siraj Raval.
The users’ information needs are static; the answer to the question
``who is the president of the united states'' never changes (Nixon is
still president, right?).
%
Finally, the Cranfield paradigm assumes that somebody, somehow can
find the right answer from thousands or millions of documents.

In addition to these specific issues, the more general issues we discuss in Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets} started even in these early days.  
%
For example, if you had a confusing or ambiguous question, the set of relevant documents would be arbitrarily fixed, and could then encourage suboptimal patterns such as thinking ``Who'' was a proper noun.

The academic community at the time was skeptical.  There’s no way
these newfangled search engines could be better than a trained
librarian armed with a card catalog!
And \citet{cuadra-68} noted reasonable criticisms: different users have
different information needs---I don't need anybody to tell me what the
capital of Maryland is, but I don't know the capital of the
Yevreyskaya Oblast; the same user has different needs---I might do the
same search twice to get the citation for Cuadra and to find a good
quote.
%
And Cuadra does have some good quotes, he correctly warns us:
\begin{quote}
  For a time, psychologists devoted their attention
not to what was important, but to what was measurable. This is a surefire
approach to a certain kind of respectability, which information
scientists now desire as much as psychologists did then, but it risks
losing the baby with the bathwater. 
\end{quote}

Despite all those flaws, the assumptions---on average---mostly hold.
%
That’s why we’re still talking about the Cranfield paradigm fifty
years later.
%
And there were some big additions to the Cranfield
paradigm.
%
Most prominent was the use of better document representations and
creating larger test collections, which was spearheaded by Karen
Sp\"arck Jones a little bit west of Cranfield at Cambridge and by Ellen
Voorhees and others at the National Institute of Standards and
Technology in Gaithersburg, Maryland.

\subsection{Which Words Matter Most in your Index?}
\label{sec:ir:tf-idf}

Weighting words gives us a practical way to search without first
resolving every ambiguity.  It does not tell us whether ``Who'' is a name,
but it can tell us which words distinguish one document from another.

We will talk about neural representations later when we talk about
models like \abr{bert} (Section~\ref{sec:methods:deep-retrieval}).
%
Those representations are called \emph{dense}---not because they're
dumb, they're quite clever---but they pack a lot of information into
a small packet of computer memory.
%
In contrast, the representations that Karen Sp\"arck Jones came up with
are \emph{sparse}: they could have an entry for every word in your
language, but for any given question, you're only going to have a
handful of words present.

And because ``word'' is ambiguous, we'll call this ``term frequency''
instead.
%
The entry for a question has a higher weight for a word the more often
it appears.
%
E.g., if the question is:
\question{Which of the members of Lincoln's cabinet did Lincoln previously serve in the House of Representatives with?}
then the words that appear most commonly are going to be more important.
%
In this case, that would be ``Lincoln''\dots but another seemingly
important is ``the'' since it also appears just as often.

The great intuition of Sp\"arck Jones is that while you and I know that
``the'' is unimportant, and you could make a list of such unimportant
term (often called a ``stop'' list in the lingo), that list is going
to be brittle.
%
For example, ``resolved'' is an unimportant term in legislative
contexts.
%
The thing that ties together unimportant term is that they appear in
many different questions.

Mathematically, you want to downweight terms that appear in many
documents.  Sp\"arck Jones motivated inverse document frequency as
\begin{quote}
[placing] greater emphasis on the value of a term as a means
of distinguishing one document from another than on its value as an indication of the content of the document itself
\end{quote}
%
In other words, you want to combine the term frequency (this term is
important to the document: it appears often) with the
inverse document frequency (this term is important overall: it only appears in a few places).
%
The most common way to do this~\citep[there are countless combinations with
funny names like \abrcamel{bm}{25}]{robertson-09}; the details are important in
practice but not for understanding the theory.
%
The most common way to do this is to weight a term~$w$ in document~$d$
as number of times~$w$ is in document~$d$ relative to the total number
of terms in~$d$, and inversely by the number of documents the word~$w$
appears in~$N_d$.
%
The term frequency is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{tf}(w,d) = \frac{n(w,d)}{\sum_{w' \in d} n(w',d)},
\end{equation}
where the denominator sums over all of the other words in the
document, and the inverse document frequency is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{idf}(w) = \log \frac{N}{N_d},
\end{equation}
where we use $\log$ to not so carefully distinguish very frequent
words (``the'') from fairly frequent words (``very''); both are about
as unimportant.

But this only gives the \emph{representation} of a document.
%
How does this go from a query to finding relevant answers?
%
Given a query representation $\vec q$---a vector with a sparse entry
for every term---and a document representation $\vec d$---also a
vector with a sparse entry for every term---to find the similarity, we
see how similar they are.
%
The more words that overlap, the higher the similarity.
%
Moreover, the higher the weight of the words, the more important their
contribution to the similarity.
%
To turn these intuitions into mathematics, we multiply each of the
terms' weights and add them all up:
\begin{equation}
  \mbox{similarity}(\vec q, \vec d) = \sum_t \vec q_t \cdot \vec d_t.
  \label{eq:ir:dot-product}
\end{equation}
This operation is called a dot product.  Keep it in mind: in
Section~\ref{sec:methods:deep-retrieval}, it will compare learned question
and passage vectors in dense retrieval and help a Transformer's attention
mechanism match queries to keys before combining their values.
%
Because most terms' weight will be zero (they don't appear in either place), this is essentially looking
for overlap of words that are important to the document but that don't
appear in many different documents.

%jbg(todo): Consider adding a figure to illustrate the dot product

These took the theoretical insights of the original
Cranfield experiments and made them into something that could actually
work.  Again, this was revolutionary: as a result the British
Computing Society named their annual award for researchers in this
area after Karen Sp\"arck Jones.

\section{Old-Fashioned \abr{ai}'s Search for Meaning}
\label{sec:ir:gofai-qa}

Finding a relevant document leaves the reader to interpret it; producing
an answer makes the computer responsible for that interpretation.
%
The Cranfield paradigm gives us a way to evaluate either task: build a
collection of questions, decide what would count as success, and compare
systems on the same examples.
%
But the early approaches to generating answers went beyond counting
words.  They used logic and representations of the world to work out
what a question meant.\footnote{This is sometimes called ``good old-fashioned
  \abr{ai}'', or \abr{gofai} for short.}

Arguably, the history of computer question answering begins with
answering questions about the American sport
of baseball.
%
Since one of my big interests is comparing human vs. computer ability, it's
worth comparing it to the state of the art in human question
answering in the mid-twentieth century~\citep{sedman-11}:
\begin{quote}
Abbott: Goofy, huh? Now let's see. We have on the bags - we have Who's on first,
What's on second, I Don't Know's on third. \\
Costello: That's what I wanna find out. \\
Abbott: I say Who's on first, What's on second, I Don't Know's on
third--- \\
Costello: You know the fellows' names? \\
Abbott: Certainly! \\
Costello: Well then who's on first? \\
Abbott: Yes! \\
Costello: I mean the fellow's name! \\
Abbott: Who! \\
Costello: The guy on first! \\
Abbott: Who! \\
Costello: The first baseman! \\
Abbott: Who! \\
Costello: The guy playing first! \\
Abbott: Who is on first! \\
Costello: Now whaddya askin' me for? \\
Abbott: I'm telling you Who is on first. \citep[pages 1002--1004]{light-16}
\end{quote}

The joke turns on a failure to agree on what kind of answer is being
requested: a person's name or an explanation of a question word.
Let’s take a look at the \abr{baseball} system
from \abr{mit}.
%
Despite the odd phrasing, the \abr{baseball} system
 is essentially a natural language
interface to an existing database~\citep{green-61}.

It does this by taking each word in the question,
trying to match it to a field in its database.
%
The challenge then is to figure out what
the question is looking for, which is usually
%
found in the question word (this is quite a bit simpler than the lexical
answer type analysis of the Watson system
(Section~\ref{sec:watson:feature-engineering}), where you need to figure out
things like ``This Argentinian Author'' could be Borges, Cortazar, or
Puig).
%
For baseball, the scope is relatively narrow: a team, a location, or a
number (number of runs or wins).

Like the search over large document collections the \abr{baseball}
system specifies what it is looking for using what it calls a
specification list.
%
If the question is \question{Where did the Red Sox play on July 7?}
the specification is
\begin{verbatim}
Place = ?
 Team = Red Sox
Month = July
  Day = 7 
\end{verbatim}
and then it finds all of the corresponding records where the Red Sox
played a game on July 7 and uses that to construct an answer.

If you know a database language like \abr{sql}, you recognize the shape
of this query: \texttt{SELECT} a field \texttt{WHERE} the other fields
match.  \abr{sql}'s predecessor, \abr{sequel}, came later~\citep{chamberlin-74},
but the comparison makes the division of labor clear: interpreting the
question determines the query, and the database supplies the answer.
%
This isn’t even taxing the full breadth
of \abr{sql}.
%
To go the next step, we turn to a system called
\abr{lunar}~\citep{woods-71}.

Which, as you might guess, was about the moon.
%
It could answer much more complicated questions,
such as those that require averages or averages
%
filtered by some criterion.
%
It can do this by converting the questions
into a rich, recursive grammar.
%
But first, it needs to analyze the grammatical
structure of the input question.

For example, the question \question{How many samples are there?} is analyzed
with the following parts of speech:
\begin{verbatim}
S Q
  NP DET HOWMANY
     N SAMPLE
     NU PL
  AUX TNS PRESENT
  VP V EXIST
\end{verbatim}
%
Unlike \abr{baseball} it can handle previous contexts (like
conversational \abr{qa}, Section~\ref{sec:formats:conversation}).


After that syntactic analysis, it then gets
turned into a logical form that can be executed
%
against its database.

In \currentyear{}, it’s probably not worth going into
that.
%
Our databases don’t look like this anymore.
%
One thing that I found interesting in rereading
this in the perspective of the 21st century,
%
is that it also has something that looks like
an \abr{ir} component, although it’s explicitly
%
looking for documents tagged with a particular
topic, unlike doing something like tf-idf
%
retrieval.

The last old-school \abr{qa} system takes interpretation into a world the
user can change: \abr{shrdlu}.  Terry Winograd described it in his
thesis~\citep{winograd-71}; his name will return with the Winograd Schema
Challenge in Section~\ref{sec:leaderboards:adversarial}.  Unlike
\abr{baseball} and \abr{lunar}, however, its name needs some explanation.

The name begins with a machine for printing words rather than
understanding them.  Since you're reading a book printed\footnote{Given the
  proliferation of reading on electronic screens, you're probably not reading this on a dead tree.  But kudos to you if you are.}
in the twenty-first century, you may not know what a Linotype machine
is.  It was the ``missing link'' between movable type, where letters
had to be arranged by hand, and sending output from a computer keyboard
to a printer.  Its keyboard controlled the casting of a line of type
into a metal piece called a slug.

A mistake on a Linotype machine could leave a peculiar trace in print.
There was no backspace to undo a cast slug, so an operator could mark a
spoiled line by mashing their hand down the first two columns of keys: ETAOIN SHRDLU.
Those letters warned that human intervention was needed to keep garbage
out of the finished page.  
%
Fredric Brown borrowed them for a story about
a Linotype machine that becomes self-aware by understanding the material
it prints (Figure~\ref{fig:ir:linotype}).  The machine demands more and
more input until the protagonist feeds it Buddhist philosophy and it
achieves Nirvana.  We'll return to machines coming alive in
Chapter~\ref{ch:sci_fi}; here, the name poses a useful question about
whether processing words amounts to understanding them.

Winograd's \abr{shrdlu} makes that question concrete by grounding language
in a small, changing world.  You can move objects around in three
dimensions and ask questions about them, rather than just query a static
database.  Like \abr{lunar}, it parses sentences and consults a knowledge
base, but that knowledge base changes as the user moves the blocks.
Figure~\ref{fig:ir:shrdlu} is a later rendering; computer graphics in the
early seventies were not as advanced as they are today.

Clarification is part of answering a question in this world.  If
\abr{shrdlu} cannot tell which object you mean, it can ask a follow-up
question~\citep{winograd-71}.  
%
Instead of repeating ``Who!'' at an
increasingly frustrated Costello, a useful conversation partner can check
which interpretation is intended. 
%
That changes what we should reward:
sometimes the appropriate response is another question, a point I return
to in Chapter~\ref{ch:bad}.

  \includegraphics[width=1.0\linewidth]{figures/external/shrdlu}
  \caption{A recreation of the \abr{shrdlu} system by the author, Jordan
    Boyd-Graber.  Unlike other
    early \abr{qa} systems with a static world, you can move the
    blocks around and ask questions about them.  However, it's a very
    small world.}
  \label{fig:ir:shrdlu}
\end{figure}

The strength of these systems is also their limitation: each makes its
world small enough to interpret precisely.  \abr{baseball} answers
questions about games, \abr{lunar} about moon rocks, and \abr{shrdlu}
about its own blocks.  Within those boundaries, a database record or a
changed arrangement of blocks gives us a concrete way to check an answer.
Outside them, the assumptions that made interpretation tractable become
sources of mistakes.

``Who's on First?'' shows how a reasonable assumption can go wrong.
Costello treats ``who'' as an interrogative pronoun when Abbott intends
a proper noun.\footnote{At the risk of explaining the joke too much,
  ``I Don't Know'' can likewise name a player rather than express a lack
  of knowledge.  The difficulty is deciding what a speaker is doing with
  the words, not merely recognizing the words themselves.}
A question parser that always treats ``who'' as a request for a person
would make the same mistake.  Natural language gives us good reasons to
prefer an interpretation, but it also gives us exceptions.

The older systems are small and brittle: when they work, they work really
well, but their carefully bounded worlds do not cover random questions
from people on the Internet.  That doesn't make logic, semantic parsing,
or knowledge bases obsolete.  It makes interpretation itself something
to evaluate: did the system choose the right reading, and did its answer
help the user?  A fixed test collection lets us compare approaches on
those questions, provided we remember whose interpretations it records.

\section{Making Hide and Seek (in Documents) a Professional Game}
\label{sec:ir:nist}

The lasting contribution of Cranfield was a way to make disagreements
about good answers testable.  To see that approach grow, we need to leave
central England for the American National Institute of Standards and
Technology, \abr{nist}.  Its Text REtrieval Conference, \abr{trec}, began
in 1992 and brought researchers together to compare systems using common
documents, questions, and evaluation procedures~\citep{nist-trec}.

Shared evaluations made progress visible across research groups.
\abr{nist}'s work extended the Cranfield approach through years of
building and refining test collections~\citep{voorhees-19}.  The resulting
comparisons were precursors to the leaderboards we will examine in
Chapter~\ref{ch:leaderboards}: a common task and scoring procedure made
it possible to put different systems' results side by side.  But agreeing
on a score still required agreeing on what counted as success.

The individual \abr{trec} tracks also made that agreement specific to a
task.  Finding a relevant document is different from finding an answer
inside it: the question answering track asked systems to return short
passages containing answers~\citep{voorhees1999trec8}.  We'll meet that
format again in Chapter~\ref{ch:datasets}.  These tasks should also look
familiar when we reach the language models in Chapter~\ref{ch:methods}:
collections of inputs paired with desired outputs can supply examples
for fine-tuning, as well as tests of performance~\citep{raffel-20,wei-22}.
The connection is the task design; a held-out evaluation set still needs
to stay separate from training data.

Reusable collections let us improve systems without running a new user
study for every change.  They also make it tempting to optimize the score
and forget the user.  Repeatedly tuning against a benchmark can overfit
its particular questions, while the people represented in a collection
may not be the people who need the resulting system.  These are central
problems for the leaderboards in Chapter~\ref{ch:leaderboards}, not reasons
to give up on evaluation.  Cleverdon's goal as a librarian was to help
people find information; a good evaluation should keep us accountable
to that goal.

But what if Costello's question about Abbott's team wasn't seeking information
but checking how well Abbott knew his players or how well Abbott managed the team.
%
Motivated by mythology and the Turing test, the next chapter ponders when questions themselves become a deliberate
instrument for testing what someone knows.  Who asks them, what they mean,
and what makes an answer acceptable will matter just as much as how we
compute the score.
